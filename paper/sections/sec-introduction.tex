\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

AI tools can now produce substantial Lean developments under human prompt design~\cite{ilin2026semiautonomousvml, avigad2026mathematiciansageai}. This shifts the practical question of how a formalization is built, and with it, the methodological question of what a formalization is actually for. Lean is built on dependent type theory, in which the Curry-Howard correspondence identifies proofs with terms and mathematical statements with types~\cite{deMoura2021Lean4}. In this sense, the formal development of a proof is the proof itself. The major formalization successes of the last five years~\cite{lean_liquid, vandoorn2023sphereeversion, Anderson_Formalization_of_the_2023, becker2025carleson_blueprint, bolan2025equationaltheoriesprojectadvancing, flt_lean} have been carried out alongside reflective accounts of the practice of building such developments~\cite{Avigad2023MathematicsAT, avigad2026mathematiciansageai}.

We report a case study that takes the Curry-Howard correspondence as its working premise: the formal Lean development is the mathematics, and the prose only renders it. There is no technical novelty in what we do, as there is now a growing literature that examines the methodological consequences of treating formal developments as designed mathematical artifacts~\cite{commelin2023abstractionboundariesspecdriven, baanen2025growing, vandoorn2020maintaining, avigad2024anatomyformalproof, ilin2026semiautonomousvml}. But Section~\ref{sec:methodology} looks at the practices that naturally follow from this constraint. A network analysis of \texttt{Mathlib}~\cite{li2026networkstructuremathlib} shows its structure is heavily infrastructural (\emph{e.g.}, coercions and typeclasses) rather than strictly mathematical. The practices below apply this library-wide distinction to individual developments. We tag every declaration as mathematical content or \emph{engineering tax}, that is, scaffolding with no mathematical counterpart. We gather this
overhead into a standalone Foundation layer that the mathematical
layer reaches only through clean interfaces. We enforce a naming
discipline under which every declaration reads aloud as a mathematical
statement. Under this arrangement the authors write no code, but they
steer the formalization entirely through prompt design and
mathematical judgment. The central principle is as follows: 
\begin{center}

\emph{the code must read as mathematics.}

\end{center}

The authors' contribution was strictly confined to mathematical judgment exercised through prompt design. For the broader question of how human judgment integrates with AI in mathematical work, see~\cite{klowden2026mathematicalmethodshumanthought}. A complementary direction is semi-autonomous mathematical \emph{discovery}, in which AI systems generate and evaluate conjectures under human supervision~\cite{Feng2026SemiAutonomousMD}. Our project operates in the distinct regime of formalization rather than discovery. This inversion is a deliberate test of how far the methodology can be pushed. We make prompt design the primary object of methodological inquiry, since prompts serve as the sole human channel into the formal artifact.

As a case study, we characterize the digraphs of real-orthogonal upper Hessenberg matrices. A \emph{marshmallow}, in Erd\H{o}s's sense, is a small result that offers a
moment of intellectual reward but no deep mathematical consequences. (The choice of problem here is driven by the second author's 
curiosity, returning after twenty-five years to a problem from their 
doctoral years~\cite{severini2003a}, and by an honest assessment of their  mathematical strength, then and now.) 
Nothing in the methodology discussed here depends on the problem being a marshmallow. If anything, keeping engineering tax out of the mathematical narrative matters more in a large formalization, since that is where the narrative is easiest to lose.

\begin{theorem}[Classification]\label{thm:main}
Fix $n \ge 2$. An unreduced real orthogonal upper Hessenberg matrix
$Q \in \R^{n \times n}$ factors canonically as a product of $n - 1$ adjacent
Givens rotations, each determined by a single parameter
$\alpha_k \in (0, 1]$. The zero--nonzero pattern of $Q$ depends only on which
parameters satisfy $\alpha_k < 1$; this subset
$S = \{k \in [n-1] : \alpha_k < 1\}$ completely determines the support
digraph $D(Q)$. Specifically:
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textup{(\arabic*)}]
\item \textup{(Bridge)}
  $D(Q) = D_n(S)$, a digraph on $[n]$ consisting of the directed Hamilton
  cycle $1 \to n \to n{-}1 \to \cdots \to 2 \to 1$ together with
  ``overlay'' arcs determined by~$S$ (Definition~\ref{def:S}).
\item \textup{(Completeness)}
  Every subset $S \subseteq [n-1]$ arises from some such matrix.
\item \textup{(Rigidity)}
  If $|S| \ge 2$, the digraph $D_n(S)$ uniquely determines~$S$.
\end{enumerate}
The number of non-isomorphic connected support digraphs on $n$ vertices is
$2^{n-1} - \lfloor(n{-}1)/2\rfloor$.
\end{theorem}

The study of support digraphs of unitary matrices was initiated by the second
author~\cite{severini2003a}, who showed that every such digraph is
\emph{strongly quadrangular}---a necessary condition that is far from
sufficient in general. Gutin, Rafiey, Severini, and
Yeo~\cite{gutin2006} subsequently established that every
digraph of a unitary matrix contains a Hamilton cycle; further structural
constraints were obtained in~\cite{severini2003b,
lundgren2004}. The complete classification of support digraphs
of arbitrary unitary matrices remains open. The Hessenberg constraint---natural
from the viewpoint of QR eigenvalue algorithms~\cite{gragg1986,
tremain2011}---makes the problem completely tractable: the Givens factorization
reduces the zero--nonzero pattern to a single subset parameter $S$, and the
tightly constrained cyclic structure of $D_n(S)$ forces rigidity. This line of
work is also motivated by the role of support digraphs in discrete quantum
walks~\cite{braunstein2006, zhan2018}, where the topology of the
walk is determined by the support of the evolution operator.

All results stated in this paper have been formalized in
Lean~4~\cite{deMoura2021Lean4} with zero \texttt{sorry}, building on the Mathlib
library~\cite{The_mathlib_Community_2020}. The development spans two files (approximately
2\,200 lines, 48 theorems and lemmas) and was carried out semi-autonomously by an AI agent
system. Following Schmitt's proposals for transparency in AI-assisted
mathematics~\cite{schmitt2025}, we provide a detailed account of the
AI--human collaboration in Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix-ai}.
